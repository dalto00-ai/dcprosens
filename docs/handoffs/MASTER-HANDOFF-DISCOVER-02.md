# ARTICLE 11 — More FPS Is Not Always Faster Input: What Frame Generation Changes

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/11-frame-generation.webp -->

The FPS counter jumps. The camera looks smoother. Your next mouse movement still does not feel twice as quick.

That is not necessarily a fault. **Frame generation adds images to the presentation of a game; a higher displayed frame count does not, by itself, prove a proportional reduction in input latency.** When comparing settings, judge the picture and the controls separately.

## One counter, two different questions

[NVIDIA's DLSS documentation](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/technologies/dlss/) describes frame generation as creating additional frames with AI. It also describes latency-reduction technology alongside it. Those are related parts of a graphics pipeline, but they are not interchangeable measurements.

The useful distinction is between how frequently new game frames are rendered and how frequently images are presented. A generated image can improve the appearance of motion without representing an entirely new simulation update responding to your latest input.

That does not make the image worthless. Smoother scenery can be exactly what you want in an exploration game. It does mean that “more FPS” needs context when the goal is responsive aiming.

## Run two comparisons, not one

Start with a repeatable scene in a game that supports the feature. A quiet practice area is easier to compare than two unrelated online matches.

Record your resolution, upscaling mode, frame cap, synchronization settings and latency-reduction setting. Compare frame generation off and on while keeping the other settings fixed where the game permits it. If enabling the feature forces another setting to change, write that down: you are comparing two configurations, not isolating one switch.

First, look at motion. Pan past detailed scenery, thin objects and interface elements. Watch for distracting instability rather than hunting for flaws in a paused screenshot.

Second, interact. Track a target, stop a turn on a landmark, and repeat a short movement sequence. Your impression is useful for choosing a setting, but it is not a laboratory measurement of milliseconds.

If the game supplies latency telemetry, read its definition. A rendering-latency metric and a complete click-to-display measurement cover different parts of the process. Do not put them in the same comparison column.

| Record | What it helps you decide |
| --- | --- |
| Displayed FPS and the overlay used | Whether the presentation rate changed |
| Base rendered FPS, if separately available | What the underlying rendering workload is doing |
| Identically defined latency metric | Whether that measured part of the response changed |
| Visible artifacts during movement | Whether the visual trade-off bothers you |
| Your preference in the same practice task | Which configuration you actually want to use |

## Avoid changing your sensitivity to compensate

If the controls seem delayed, raising sensitivity changes turn distance. It does not identify or remove the cause of the delay. Keep sensitivity stable during the comparison so that you are not learning a new mouse movement at the same time.

For a competitive match, you might prefer one configuration; for a visually demanding single-player game, another. Neither decision requires calling every generated frame “fake” or assuming every larger counter is automatically better.

## Keep the result specific

Save a note with the game, graphics settings and hardware. A result from one title is not a universal verdict on every implementation, and a future game update may change the comparison.

The next time a performance screenshot catches your attention, ask one extra question: what happened to response and image quality at those settings? The FPS number becomes more useful once it stops carrying the entire argument.

For sensitivity changes after your graphics test, use the [DCPROSENS converter](https://dcprosens.com/) as a starting calculation, then verify the result in the game.

# ARTICLE 12 — Your High-Refresh Monitor May Be Running Below Its Rated Speed

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/12-monitor-refresh-rate.webp -->

A monitor can have a high refresh rate printed on its box while your computer is sending it a lower-rate signal. Before deciding the upgrade made little difference, check the active display setting.

**In Windows, open Settings → System → Display → Advanced display, choose the correct screen, and inspect its refresh rate.** The monitor's advertised maximum is a capability, not confirmation of the mode currently in use.

## Check the screen you actually play on

[Microsoft's display guidance](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/hardware/display-graphics/change-the-refresh-rate-on-your-monitor-in-windows) explains where to change refresh rate and notes that available options depend on the display. Some choices can also require a resolution change.

On a multi-monitor desk, it is easy to inspect the wrong screen. Identify the gaming display first. Then note the active resolution and refresh rate together instead of reading only the largest number in a dropdown.

For scale, a 60 Hz refresh interval is about 16.67 milliseconds; 144 Hz is about 6.94 milliseconds; 240 Hz is about 4.17 milliseconds. These are calculated as 1,000 divided by Hz. They describe refresh intervals, not complete input latency, pixel response time or a promise that a game produces that many fresh frames.

## When the expected option is missing

Do not start by forcing an undocumented custom mode. Work through the signal path using your monitor and computer documentation.

- Check which input on the monitor is connected.
- Check whether a dock, adapter or switch sits between the computer and display.
- Look up the supported resolution-and-refresh combination for that connection.
- Check the monitor's own settings for any model-specific mode requirement.
- Revisit Windows after a cable or connection change.

The relevant question is whether the complete connection supports the desired mode. A connector's shape alone does not answer it, and a cable advertised with a large number does not establish every device's capabilities.

## Three rates that should not share one label

Your monitor refresh setting, the game's rendered frame rate and a variable-refresh range answer different questions. A display configured for a high maximum does not force a demanding game to render at that speed.

There is also Windows Dynamic Refresh Rate. Microsoft describes DRR as changing refresh rate according to activity on supported systems and notes that it may limit some games' maximum rate. If that matches your symptom, compare with DRR disabled. Do not assume DRR and a game's variable-refresh behavior are the same setting.

## A short verification you can keep

Make a small setup record after changing the mode:

| Check | Your entry |
| --- | --- |

Use a familiar scene to check comfort and motion. You do not need to buy anything to establish whether the current setup is configured correctly.

If the setting was already right, that is still a useful result. It removes one uncertainty and gives you a reason to examine the game's performance instead of repeatedly changing display options.

A high-refresh monitor earns its place through the experience it delivers in your setup. Verifying the active mode is the first, simplest part of that assessment.

# ARTICLE 13 — Your Game Clips Have a Storage Budget—Check It Before Your Next Session

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/13-game-recording-storage.webp -->

The best moment of the evening is over in a few seconds. Keeping the whole evening at high recording quality can take much more room than the moment itself.

**Give background recording a deliberate storage budget: decide how far back you need to capture, where the files belong and which highlights you want to keep.** You can make those decisions before the next session instead of during a low-disk-space warning.

## Start with the amount of history you need

A player saving occasional highlights has a different need from someone reviewing complete matches. Write down which of those jobs the recording is supposed to do.

For highlights, the important question is how much lead-in makes a clip understandable. For analysis, the question is whether you need the entire round, match or session. Recording everything indefinitely is a storage policy too—just one you may not have chosen intentionally.

Steam's [Game Recording help](https://help.steampowered.com/en/faqs/view/23B7-49AD-4A28-9590) identifies controls for recording quality, duration and storage. Inspect the options exposed by your installed client; wording and available choices can change.

## A simple estimate before you press record

If you know the average video bitrate, an approximate video-only size is:

**Size in decimal MB = bitrate in Mbps × duration in seconds ÷ 8.**

For an illustrative 20 Mbps recording lasting ten minutes, that is 20 × 600 ÷ 8 = 1,500 MB, or 1.5 GB. This is arithmetic, not a measured Steam preset. Audio, container overhead and a variable bitrate can change the actual file size.

| Illustrative video bitrate | Ten minutes | One hour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 Mbps | 0.75 GB | 4.5 GB |
| 20 Mbps | 1.5 GB | 9 GB |
| 40 Mbps | 3 GB | 18 GB |

The table does not tell you which quality setting to choose. It shows why a seemingly small change in recording duration can matter when it is repeated across many sessions.

## Run a sample instead of guessing

Record ten minutes of a game you actually play. Include fast movement and a visually busy section, then inspect the exported result at the size you expect viewers to watch.

Ask whether the action is clear, whether the audio is useful and whether the file size fits your plan. If the clip is only going to a short social post, evaluate that output as well as the full-size desktop version.

Keep the test controlled if you are checking performance: repeat a comparable scene with recording off and on, without also changing graphics settings. A difference between two unrelated matches is not enough to blame recording.

## Separate a rolling buffer from an archive

Temporary history and a saved highlight serve different purposes. Check what the client preserves, what it replaces and what an export actually creates. Do not assume a moment is permanently archived because you could rewind to it during the session.

A practical folder routine is to keep new clips in one review location, export the moments worth retaining and remove unwanted copies after checking the exports. Before deleting anything, distinguish original footage from the copy you intend to publish.

For team or voice-chat recordings, also decide which audio tracks you actually need. Fewer irrelevant tracks can make editing simpler, regardless of their effect on size.

Your goal is not the smallest possible recording. It is a recording clear enough to use, long enough to contain the moment and organized well enough that you can find it afterward.

# ARTICLE 14 — A Bottleneck Percentage Is Not a PC Diagnosis

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/14-bottleneck-calculator.webp -->

A calculator gives your PC a bottleneck percentage. The number looks precise enough to turn into a shopping list: replace this processor, buy that graphics card, solve the problem.

**A hardware pairing alone cannot describe every game, scene and setting you will run. Treat a bottleneck estimate as a question to investigate, not a diagnosis or a percentage of performance you can automatically recover.**

## The missing part of the number is the workload

[Intel's technical guide to CPU and GPU bottlenecks](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/locate-and-resolve-cpu-gpu-bottlenecks.html) discusses how limiting work can change across scenes and how adjusting workload can help identify the constraint.

The practical implication is that “this PC is bottlenecked” is incomplete. What is it running, at which settings, and what performance target is it failing to reach?

A crowded simulation-heavy scene and a graphically demanding quiet scene can ask different things of the same machine. That is why a universal percentage is a poor substitute for observing the game that matters to you.

## Define the problem before the upgrade

Write one sentence that describes the actual failure. For example: “During this repeatable practice sequence, performance falls below my chosen target.” That is more useful than “my CPU is too weak.”

Record the game version, resolution, graphics preset, frame cap and test location. Use built-in or game-supported telemetry where available. Avoid adding several monitoring tools at once, because then your test environment has changed too.

If your performance already meets a frame cap comfortably, a component does not need to report maximum utilization. Spare capacity is not automatically evidence of a defective setup.

## Make a change that answers a question

Try reducing rendering resolution while leaving the scene and other settings unchanged. If performance improves substantially, that is consistent with graphics workload being important in that scene. It does not prove that every slowdown has the same cause.

If performance barely changes, you have narrowed the investigation, not identified the CPU with certainty. A frame cap, synchronization setting, another workload or a different constraint may still matter.

| Observation | Useful next question |
| --- | --- |
| Lower resolution substantially improves performance | Which visual settings are worth trading for speed? |
| Lower resolution changes little | Is a cap active, or is another part of the workload limiting progress? |
| Only one scene performs poorly | What is different about that scene? |
| Results vary widely between repeated runs | Is the test actually repeatable? |

Repeat the original configuration after the change. That return check helps reveal whether the scene, background activity or test method moved underneath you.

## Turn evidence into a spending decision

Before buying, look for benchmarks that match your intended game, settings and candidate hardware as closely as possible. Results from a different workload can provide context, but they cannot promise your exact outcome.

Also set a meaningful target. An upgrade that improves an average you already find comfortable may be less valuable to you than fixing a recurring disruption. A number should connect to an experience you care about.

The [DCPROSENS FPS calculator](https://dcprosens.com/fps-calculator/) belongs in the estimate category too. Use it to organize an initial comparison, then validate the assumptions against your own observations and relevant measurements.

A good diagnosis should tell you what to test next. If a percentage immediately tells you what to buy without explaining the workload, it has skipped the most useful part.

# ARTICLE 15 — Stretched Resolution Makes the Picture Wider, Not the Hitboxes

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/15-stretched-resolution.webp -->

A target looks wider after you switch to a stretched image. It is tempting to conclude that the game has made that target easier to hit by enlarging its hitbox.

**Display stretching changes the shape of the final picture. Stretching alone does not rewrite the game's collision geometry.** The visual result may suit your preference, but the picture and the underlying game rules are different things.

## Start with what the display is doing

[NVIDIA's display-scaling documentation](https://www.nvidia.com/content/Control-Panel-Help/vLatest/en-us/mergedProjects/Display/CS_Display_Scaling.htm) distinguishes full-screen stretching from aspect-ratio-preserving scaling and no scaling. Those options determine how an image is fitted to a display.

The hitbox conclusion here is a technical inference about that final-image operation: resizing the completed picture does not, by itself, change the geometry used by the game to decide whether a shot hits.

This distinction matters because changing an in-game resolution or aspect ratio can involve more than display scaling. A title may also change its field of view, interface layout or rendering behavior. Those effects need to be checked in that particular game.

## Why the wider target is only half the story

Consider a purely geometric example: a 4:3 picture stretched to fill a 16:9 area without preserving its aspect ratio. The horizontal-to-vertical scale ratio is (16/9) ÷ (4/3), which equals 4/3.

Objects become about 33.3% wider relative to their height in that example. The transformation applies to the picture, including the space and apparent movement around a target. It is not a special enlargement applied only to opponents.

That calculation is an illustration of scaling, not a measurement of any game's hit registration. It also does not determine what a game renders before the image reaches the display.

## Compare the setting you actually changed

Create a small record for each configuration: render resolution, selected aspect ratio, scaling mode and any available field-of-view setting. Keep mouse sensitivity fixed initially.

Choose a practice location with stable landmarks. Compare how much of the scene is visible, whether objects look distorted and whether you can read targets comfortably during movement. A stationary screenshot cannot answer every question about how a moving scene feels.

Then perform the same short aiming task in both configurations. If you are comparing your performance, repeat the test rather than treating one unusually good run as the verdict.

## Do not fix a visual adjustment with five other changes

Switching resolution, sensitivity, crosshair and graphics preset together produces a new setup, but it makes the cause of any improvement difficult to identify.

Instead, separate the decisions. First establish whether you prefer the stretched presentation. Then adjust other settings only if a specific problem remains. Write down the original values so you can return to them.

If preserving physical turn distance across games is also part of your setup, start with the [DCPROSENS sensitivity converter](https://dcprosens.com/). A conversion can help with a defined mouse-distance goal; it cannot make two different visual projections look identical.

## A preference does not need a false explanation

You may like a stretched image. You may find its target shapes easier to read, or you may dislike the distortion. Those are valid reasons to choose a display configuration.

The stronger claim—that stretching the screen enlarges the game's hitboxes—does not follow from the wider picture. Keep the preference, and give it an explanation that matches what the setting actually changes.

# ARTICLE 16 — A Pro’s Sensitivity Is a Starting Point, Not Your Setup

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/16-copying-pro-settings.webp -->

Copying a professional player's settings is easy. Copying the circumstances in which those settings work is another matter.

**A pro configuration can be a useful starting hypothesis. It is not evidence that the same numbers will solve your aiming problem.** Before adopting it, check what the values mean, where they came from and whether you can use them comfortably at your desk.

## Copy the complete reference

A sensitivity number without its game and DPI is incomplete. A value also needs context: hip-fire or scoped, the relevant input mode, and the date or source of the configuration.

Do not assume a database entry describes what someone used yesterday. Players can change settings, and a copied list can outlive its original context. Look for a dated first-person source when current settings are essential to your decision.

The [DCPROSENS pro settings page](https://dcprosens.com/pros/) can help you browse examples. Treat entries as references to verify, not certificates of an optimal setup or proof of a player's current equipment.

## Ask which problem the change is supposed to solve

“I want this player's aim” is an aspiration. “I repeatedly run out of mouse travel during turns” is a problem you can investigate.

Other useful observations include overshooting small corrections, struggling to keep a moving target centered, or lifting the mouse more often than you want. The same visible mistake can have several causes, so describe it before deciding that sensitivity is responsible.

This also prevents a familiar cycle: copy a new value, have one good match, struggle the next day and copy someone else. A configuration needs a defined test if you want to learn from it.

## Check the physical requirement

Measure actual mouse movement for a known turn in a suitable practice environment. A converter can provide an initial estimate, but an in-game check confirms what your current configuration does.

As a simple example, a linear setup measured at 40 cm for a full turn needs 20 cm for a half turn. That arithmetic does not prescribe 40 cm as an ideal sensitivity. It gives you a concrete distance to compare with your available travel.

Clear the space you realistically use, including the keyboard and anything that restricts the mouse. A large pad under a crowded desk does not necessarily provide a large movement area.

## Try a small comparison you can repeat

Keep your current setting as configuration A and the copied reference as B. Use the same map or practice task, and keep graphics, crosshair and mouse hardware unchanged.

Spend enough time with each to understand the required movement. Record a short observation after each block rather than relying only on the final score.

| Question | Useful observation |
| --- | --- |
| Can I make a comfortable large turn? | Space used and whether I must reposition |
| Can I stop on a small landmark? | Repeated overshoot or undershoot |
| Can I follow a moving target? | Where control becomes difficult |
| Can I repeat the task without discomfort? | Whether the setup remains comfortable |

These observations do not isolate talent or guarantee improvement. They help you decide whether a borrowed configuration is worth retaining, adjusting or discarding.

## Borrow the question, not the reputation

A professional setting is interesting because someone has found a use for it at a high level. That makes it worth examining, not automatically right for every player.

Use the [DCPROSENS converter](https://dcprosens.com/) when you need to translate a sensitivity reference between supported games, then check the result in the target game. The configuration becomes yours when you understand why you kept it—not when the numbers match a famous player's screenshot.

# ARTICLE 17 — Magnetic Controller Sticks Reduce One Drift Risk, Not Every Failure

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/17-magnetic-controller-sticks.webp -->

“Anti-drift” is an appealing promise if you have ever watched a character move while your thumb was off the stick. Magnetic sensing addresses a real design issue, but a controller is more than its position sensor.

**Hall-effect and TMR sticks use magnetic sensing rather than the sliding electrical contact found in potentiometer designs. That avoids one contact-wear mechanism; it does not establish that every part of the controller will last forever or that every unit will center perfectly.**

## Separate the sensor from the whole assembly

[Razer's explanation of controller stick designs](https://www.razer.com/blog/razer-controllers-built-to-last) contrasts potentiometer contact wear with magnetic sensing and discusses Hall-effect and TMR implementations. It is manufacturer material, useful for understanding the stated design but not independent proof of lifetime or comparative performance.

The broader reliability conclusion is an engineering distinction: changing the sensing method does not remove all mechanical parts, firmware behavior or calibration requirements from a controller.

That is why a sensor label is useful information without being a complete buying decision. A promising technology still has to be implemented in a product you can use comfortably.

## Ask what “drift” describes in your case

Players use the word for several observations: a character moves at rest, the camera slowly rotates, or the center position appears offset in a test. Those symptoms describe what you see, not necessarily the cause.

Before judging a controller, check whether the behavior appears in more than one supported game or test environment. Record which input path you are using and whether a game-specific setting changes the result. Do not compare two products with different software filters and assume you have isolated their sensors.

This article is not a repair procedure. If a new controller behaves unexpectedly, the manufacturer's documented calibration and support process is a better starting point than opening it.

## What to evaluate beyond the label

A useful shortlist includes the parts of ownership you will actually notice:

| Check | Why it belongs in the decision |
| --- | --- |
| Platform and connection support | A good stick does not solve incompatibility |
| Grip shape and reachable buttons | Controls need to suit your hands and use |
| Centering behavior in supported software | It shows how the complete unit behaves |
| Calibration and firmware support | It establishes the documented maintenance path |
| Return and warranty terms | They matter when an individual unit disappoints |
| Independent tests with a described method | They are more informative than an unexplained ranking |

Treat advertised accuracy or longevity figures as claims to examine. Look for what was measured, how many units were tested and which connection and firmware were used. If that information is absent, do not fill the gap with assumptions.

## Compare a repeatable movement

For a controller you already have, use a familiar practice environment. Release the sticks normally, make slow movements near center, and perform wider turns. Note whether the issue is repeatable and whether it affects the task you care about.

Avoid turning a calibration screen into a contest for the prettiest circle. A test display is evidence about a defined input behavior, not a complete measure of comfort, reliability or in-game control.

Keep the controller's baseline settings before experimenting so you can restore a known configuration.

## Buy the implementation you can evaluate

Magnetic sensing can be a meaningful reason to consider a controller. It should sit alongside compatibility, comfort, measured behavior and support—not replace them.

The useful question is not whether the box contains the newest acronym. It is whether the whole controller meets your needs, with claims specific enough that you can check them.

# ARTICLE 18 — Your Keyboard’s Most Sensitive Setting May Be Too Sensitive for You

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/18-rapid-trigger-keyboard.webp -->

A keyboard lets you choose an extremely small movement threshold. Setting it to the minimum feels like the obvious way to get everything you paid for.

But the smallest threshold is only useful if you can control it. **If your movement repeatedly stops or restarts when you do not intend it to, test a less sensitive setting before assuming your technique is the entire problem.**

## Two settings with different jobs

[Wooting's rapid-trigger explanation](https://wooting.io/rapid-trigger) describes activation and deactivation based on key movement rather than relying only on fixed travel points. The implementation and available controls vary by product.

The initial actuation setting concerns when a press first registers. Rapid-trigger behavior concerns how movement can reset and reactivate a key afterward. Adjusting both at once makes it harder to identify which change affected your control.

Also, short key travel is not the same as zero system latency. The keyboard, game and display still form a larger input process. A travel-distance option does not measure that entire chain.

## Look for a repeatable mistake

Start with one behavior you can observe in a practice environment. Perhaps holding movement feels inconsistent, or a small release leads to a new input sooner than you expect.

Describe the behavior before changing anything. “I released slightly and movement stopped” is more useful than “the keyboard feels bad.” If the problem is accidental initial presses while resting your fingers, the initial actuation setting may deserve attention separately from rapid-trigger reset.

Use the manufacturer's software or documentation to identify which control you are adjusting. Similar-looking sliders across different products do not necessarily behave identically.

## Make the comparison smaller

Save your existing profile. Change one movement key or a clearly defined group of movement keys rather than rebuilding the entire keyboard layout.

Try your current setting, then a modestly less sensitive option. Keep initial actuation unchanged if the device allows an independent comparison. Repeat the same movement sequence with both.

| Practice action | Observe |
| --- | --- |
| Hold a direction steadily | Any unintended release |
| Release deliberately | Whether the stop matches your intention |
| Press again after a small release | Any unintended repeat or missed input |
| Repeat in ordinary play | Whether the setting remains controllable outside the drill |

This is a preference test, not a measured claim that one threshold makes all players faster. The useful result is fewer unintended inputs while preserving the responsiveness you want.

## Keep unrelated features separate

Rapid trigger should not be used as a catch-all name for every keyboard feature. A device may also offer input-priority behavior, remapping or macros. Those can change what happens in ways unrelated to a key's reset distance.

When comparing profiles, record which extra features are active. Check the relevant game's or tournament's rules before enabling separate input automation. Otherwise, you may attribute a behavior to rapid trigger that came from something else.

## Give yourself a profile you can trust

After choosing a setting, keep a note of the keyboard model, profile and firmware version. If behavior changes after a software update or profile switch, that note gives you a reference.

You do not need the smallest available number to justify owning the keyboard. You need a setting that turns intended movements into inputs consistently. If backing away from the minimum makes that easier, the adjustment is doing its job.

# ARTICLE 19 — The Cleanest Crosshair Can Be the Hardest to See

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/19-crosshair-visibility.webp -->

A tiny crosshair looks excellent in a settings screenshot. Then you turn toward a bright wall during a match and spend a moment trying to find it.

**Choose a crosshair you can locate during movement, across the backgrounds you actually play against. A clean-looking preview is not enough to establish that it is usable in a match.**

## Test the scene, not just the preview

A preview usually gives you a controlled background. A match gives you changing light, architecture, effects and targets at different distances. A design that looks precise in the menu may not remain obvious in those conditions.

Start with three locations in a practice environment: a bright surface, a dark area and a visually busy scene. Turn between landmarks at your normal speed. Ask whether you can find the center immediately without deliberately searching for the crosshair.

This is an individual visibility check, not a claim that one color works best for every map or every person's vision.

## Change the property that matches the problem

If the crosshair blends into a background, compare color or an outline where the game supports it. If it covers the target detail you need, compare thickness, length or center-dot size. Those are different problems and may need different changes.

Riot's [VALORANT patch notes 5.04](https://playvalorant.com/en-us/news/game-updates/valorant-patch-notes-5-04/) documented expanded crosshair customization, including custom colors and independent line adjustments. That is historical background about available design controls, not a claim about a new patch or the exact menu layout in your current build.

Use the options currently offered by your game. Do not assume a numeric preset transfers identically to another title or resolution.

| What you notice | A focused comparison |
| --- | --- |
| Crosshair vanishes on pale surfaces | Compare an outline or a contrasting color |
| Center covers distant target detail | Compare a smaller center mark or less thickness |
| Shape is hard to locate during turns | Compare slightly more visible line length or thickness |
| One map is comfortable but another is not | Repeat the background check on both |

Treat each change as a candidate. A thicker line may solve visibility while introducing obstruction, so return to the distant target after testing the bright wall.

## Keep one reference profile

Save the original profile or code before making changes. Create one alternative, then compare the two in the same environment. Ten simultaneous experiments make it easy to lose track of what helped.

Keep sensitivity and display settings steady. If you change the image scale and crosshair together, you will not know which change altered the apparent size or feel.

You can sketch a candidate with the [DCPROSENS crosshair generator](https://dcprosens.com/crosshair/). Confirm its appearance inside the game before keeping it; a browser preview is a design aid, not a substitute for the final rendering.

## Judge the task instead of the screenshot

The crosshair has to remain useful when your attention is on a target. It does not need to win a comparison of the smallest possible shapes.

After trying a candidate, write down the specific improvement: easier to locate on a bright wall, less obstruction at distance, or no meaningful difference. A score alone can hide the reason you preferred it.

A slightly more visible crosshair may be the right choice even if it looks less minimal in a shared screenshot. The final test is whether you can use it without repeatedly noticing the effort of finding it.

# ARTICLE 20 — Your Reaction-Time Score Measures More Than Your Reflexes

<!-- BEGIN PUBLIC ARTICLE COPY: use the title above as the page H1 without the ARTICLE label. Cover: images/20-reaction-time-score.webp -->

Someone shares an astonishing reaction-time screenshot. You try the same kind of test, get a larger number and wonder whether your reflexes are holding back your gaming.

**A browser reaction-time score is a result from a person using a particular device, display, browser and test. It is not a hardware-free measurement of talent, and one unusually fast attempt is not a reliable description of your usual performance.**

## The signal has to travel

A visual task starts with a software event, reaches the display, prompts your response and receives an input through the device and software again. A browser test's timestamps do not automatically separate every step in that path.

[NVIDIA's system-latency guide](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/guides/gfecnt/202010/system-latency-optimization-guide/) describes the broader input-to-display pipeline. Applying that distinction here is an inference: a web score should not be treated as if the surrounding system contributes nothing to the result.

This is also why comparing a phone touch test with a desktop mouse test does not isolate a difference between the two people using them.

## Decide what you want to compare

There are two useful questions: “How consistent am I on this setup?” and “Did a controlled setup change alter the result?” They require different comparisons.

For the first, keep the device and environment stable. For the second, change one factor and return to the baseline afterward. If you switch the monitor, mouse, browser and test website together, any difference belongs to the complete configuration.

Neither approach makes a casual browser task a medical assessment or a complete measure of competitive skill. A match asks you to interpret events and choose actions, not only click after a simple signal.

## Use a small, honest recording routine

Choose one test and follow its instructions. Do a short familiarization block before recording a session. Then collect a fixed number of valid attempts rather than stopping as soon as you get a number worth sharing.

Twenty valid attempts is a practical editorial suggestion for a personal log, not a scientifically validated minimum. If the test flags an early click, record that separately; do not turn an anticipated response into a successful reaction.

Write down the test site, browser, input device and active display refresh rate. Keep the conditions similar when you return. Do not start deleting ordinary slow attempts simply because they make the result less impressive.

## Summarize more than the best click

For a small set, the median is a useful descriptive companion to the full results. Sort the values; with twenty attempts, average the tenth and eleventh values. Also retain the number of early clicks and the individual results so you can see the spread.

This does not establish a population percentile or a confidence interval. It simply avoids letting one best attempt speak for the entire session.

The [DCPROSENS reaction-time test](https://dcprosens.com/reaction-time-test/) offers a short browser task and reports an average across its attempt set. Keep that average labeled as an average; calculate a median only when you have the underlying individual values. Do not rename one statistic as the other.

## Share the conditions with the number

A useful post can say which setup you used, how many attempts you recorded and which statistic you are reporting. That turns a screenshot into something readers can interpret.

Use the result to ask better questions about your own consistency and setup. It should not decide whether you are talented enough to play a game you enjoy.
